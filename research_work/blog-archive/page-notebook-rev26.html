Gravity that streams

One law for spinning galaxies, bending light, galaxy clusters and colliding clusters. Built from first principles, tested on public data, with no dark matter: three constants fitted to galaxies and clusters, and one length set by the Solar System.

New in revision 26: keeping warm matter in tune. Revision 25 left one link missing: when a source warms up, its pieces fall out of tune, distant matter can’t keep step with its wave, and the pull doesn’t grow with the heat. What pulls harder than it should, and why

Stars at the edges of galaxies orbit too fast. Light bends too much around galaxies. Clusters hold their hot gas with 4 to 27 times the pull their visible matter provides. And when two clusters collide, the extra pull follows the galaxies, not the gas, even though the gas outweighs the galaxies ten to fifty times.

Our answer, in plain words:

  • Every piece of ordinary matter feeds a faint, streaming companion to its gravity.
  • Orderly matter feeds it in step, so contributions from opposite sides cancel, just as Newton’s pulls do. What survives falls off slowly enough to keep a galaxy’s outer stars moving fast. (Revision 20: this follows from energy conservation.)
  • Matter whose parts move randomly and freely — stars in elliptical galaxies, galaxies in clusters — feeds it out of step. Nothing cancels, so it pulls much harder. (Revision 19: these two rules are now set by data. Of three natural ways the companion could add up, only this one fits both galaxies and clusters.)
  • Gas is not “hot” in this sense. Its particles collide so often that the companion sees them as sitting still. So in a collision the extra pull stays with the galaxies.
  • Strong gravity holds the companion back, so the Solar System feels nothing.
  • The companion is slow, and keeps moving the way its source moved. After a collision each cluster is still wrapped in the companion it had before. That is why the Bullet Cluster’s smaller half still bends light like the whole cluster it used to be.

The problem

Four measurements, one explanation

  • Rotation. Across 149 galaxies and 3,150 measured speeds (the SPARC catalogue), Newton’s law applied to the visible stars and gas misses by 45.6 km/s on average. Far from the centre, the speeds stay flat instead of falling.
  • Lensing. Galaxies and clusters bend background light more than their visible matter can.
  • Clusters. In 12 well-measured clusters (X-COP), Newton misses the mass that holds the hot gas by a factor of about 9.
  • Collisions. In the Bullet Cluster, two clusters passed through each other about 150 million years ago. The galaxies sailed on; the gas collided and stayed in the middle. The lensing sits on the galaxies.

Three standard responses. Dark matter adds invisible mass, tuned object by object; the particle has never been detected. MOND changes the law at low accelerations; it predicts galaxies well but misses clusters by a factor of about 3 and cannot move a collision’s lensing onto the galaxies. Newton alone fails everywhere outside the Solar System.

Our ground rules. No dark matter. No MOND, or anything derived from it, as an input. No plain Newton as the answer. No expanding universe. Every formula passes an automatic check that it is not secretly any of the three. We work from first principles, to a toy model, to real data; when a test fails we ask what would have to change.

The law

Built one idea at a time

  1. Matter feeds a companion

    Every kilogram of ordinary matter steadily feeds a tiny power, ℓ watts per kilogram, into a companion to its gravity, which streams outward at a speed u. This is the project’s founding intuition — gravity produced and carried, not simply attached — and it is the only new ingredient.

  2. Orderly matter adds up the way Newton does

    Energy streaming from a source spreads over a sphere, so its flux falls as 1/distance², the same geometry as Newton’s pull. Added arrow by arrow, the flows from orderly matter point exactly along Newton’s pull, and opposite contributions cancel. We check this identity to 2 parts in 1015.

    F_coherent = (ℓ / 4πG) · g_N

  3. The companion pulls with its strength

    Give the companion an energy density A²/8πG — the same form as a gravitational field’s — and let its pull equal its amplitude A. Balancing what streams out through a sphere against what the mass inside feeds in fixes the pull:

    4πr² · u · A²/8πG = ℓM ⇒ A = √(G M a) / r, a = 2ℓ/u

    A pull that falls as 1/r gives an orbital speed that is the same at every radius — a flat rotation curve — and the observed rule v4 = G M a. The constant a is not a new constant of nature: it is twice the power per kilogram divided by the companion’s speed. The fitted power, 5.3 × 10−6 W/kg, amounts to two and a half thousandths of one percent of a kilogram’s rest energy over 13 billion years, the age of the oldest stars.

  4. Strong gravity holds the companion back

    Near the Sun, gravity is billions of times stronger than in a galaxy’s outskirts. If the companion pulled there, planetary orbits would show it. So it is held back where ordinary gravity is strong and released where it is weak. At every planet the released fraction is zero.

    released fraction = exp( −|g_N| / g_d ), g_d = 2.03 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s²

    Breaking free takes time (revision 18). The companion is not released the instant it leaves the strong-pull zone. It has to travel about 0.15 parsec, 30,000 times the Earth–Sun distance or about 900 years at its speed, to break free. Cassini’s radio tracking of Saturn requires this. Even the smallest galaxies are a thousand times larger, so nothing there changes.

    released share builds up as 1 − exp( −distance from its source / L ), L = 0.15 pc

  5. Random motion scrambles it, and scrambled parts don’t cancel

    A moving source Doppler-shifts what it emits. In a population whose parts move randomly with spread σ, contributions drift out of step and add as plain totals instead of cancelling arrows. The Doppler boost, averaged over random directions, also makes them feed harder, by 3σ²/u². In a simulation, in-step emitters gave the arrow sum (160.50 against 160.49 predicted) and scrambled ones the plain total (210.55 against 209.20). Inside a big cloud Newton’s pulls largely cancel but the plain total does not — exactly the extra pull clusters need. Revision 22: joined with the pull in one experiment, scrambling alone makes the pull weaker. The heat term stands on the data; its mechanism is a release of companion that cold matter holds back (below).

    S = G ∫ k ρ / d², heat weight k = 3σ² / u²

  6. Collisions switch the scrambling off

    This is the idea that answers colliding clusters. Laboratory physics has a known exception to Doppler scrambling: an emitter that changes direction many times before it moves one wavelength shows no Doppler effect at all. It is called Dicke narrowing (1953); its cousin, the Mössbauer effect, is why atoms locked in a crystal emit perfectly sharp lines. Gas particles collide, and in clusters spiral on magnetic fields, constantly. Stars never collide.

So only stars and galaxies carry the heat term; gas still counts in full as mass.

  7. It pulls along its net flow

    Out-of-step waves don’t add their strengths as arrows, but their energy flows still do. So the extra pull points along Newton’s pull plus the heat-weighted flow of the free-moving matter, mixed in proportion to their sizes. In a round galaxy or cluster both point to the centre and nothing changes. In a collision the gas dominates Newton’s pull but the galaxies dominate the heat-weighted flow, so the pull — and the lensing — bends toward the galaxies.

    direction = (g_N + g_hot) / (|g_N| + |g_hot|)

  8. It is slow, and it remembers

    Nothing new is added here; two things follow from what we have. It is slow: at 169 km/s it takes about 580 million years to travel 100 kpc, so most of the companion around a galaxy or cluster today was given off long ago. It keeps moving the way its source moved, like a ball thrown from a moving train. It has to: galaxies move through space faster than the companion itself, and if it moved through a fixed background, a galaxy’s gravity would depend on its speed. All galaxies obey one rotation law, so it doesn’t.

    For anything settled, nothing changes. In a collision it matters: the galaxies sail on and carry the old companion with them, while around the stopped gas a fresh companion has had time to build up only a short way. Each cluster is still wrapped in the companion of the whole cluster it was before.

First principles

Where the pull comes from, and how the companion adds up

Three rules of the law describe how the companion behaves: it pulls with its strength; orderly matter’s contributions cancel as Newton’s pulls do; scrambled contributions add as plain totals. Revision 19 takes the first steps toward deriving them, and tests the alternatives on the data.

The model. The companion is a wave. Every piece of matter is a small, self-powered emitter of it, like a clock that ticks by itself, and its ticking falls into step with the wave passing it. The only interaction is local: each emitter is pushed by the slope of the wave where it sits.

The result is like pushing a swing.

  • A quarter beat ahead of the passing wave, an emitter adds energy to it, like a well-timed push on a swing. That energy flows away from the source, so the emitter recoils toward it. The pull is exactly proportional to the passing wave’s strength, at every distance: the law’s rule, derived rather than assumed. A full 3D simulation of the wave, which knows nothing of the formula, agrees to within 4%.
  • In step with the wave, the pull falls as 1/distance², like Newton’s.
  • A quarter beat behind, the emitter takes energy from the wave, like catching a swing, and is pushed away.

So the microscopic model must have matter feed the companion, as the medium of a laser feeds its beam; each tiny emitter must lock at its own slightly different pitch, or galaxies would follow the wrong mass–speed rule; and the wave’s crests must move at most 1/20 as fast as its energy.

The pull, read locally: it equals the companion’s local strength times how quickly the crests sweep past. Where companion arrives from several directions, the crests partly stand still, as on a guitar string, and the pull weakens: add a counter-wave of half the strength and the pull drops to 81%, although there is a quarter more companion.

There the medium sets the waves’ strength; the companion’s must instead fall off away from its source. A model that does both is the next step, graded on the same galaxies, clusters and Milky Way. Revision 20: why orderly matter adds up like Newton

The test above picked the rule out of three. Energy conservation now says why it must be so, in four steps:

  1. No energy is lost on the way. Every watt matter feeds into the companion flows outward, so through any closed surface the power flowing out equals the power fed in by the matter inside.
  2. Matter feels the full pull only from one stream whose crests sweep past at the full rate (above). The stream’s energy is then its energy flow divided by its speed, and the pull goes as the square root of that flow.
  3. No whirlpools: suppose the flow has no swirl.
  4. Then only one flow is possible, the pattern of Newton’s field lines. So the extra pull goes as the square root of Newton’s pull: the law’s rule, derived.

The alternative breaks energy conservation. “One stream, nothing cancels” would carry more power out of a region than its matter puts in: 1.5 times at the edge of a uniform ball and 12 times at a quarter of its radius; in the Milky Way model 1.70 times through a sphere of 4 kpc and 1.36 times through one of 8.2 kpc.

Revision 22: the pull and the scrambling in one experiment

You asked us to join the two working pieces, the pull above and the scrambling of the idea list, in one simulation with the same matter and the same rules, changing only how the matter moves, with test bodies that feel nothing but the wave where they sit. Picture 100 tiny singers in a ball about two companion wavelengths across. Each sings the companion wave, listens to the wave around it and keeps re-timing itself a fixed amount ahead of, or behind, what it hears; 32 more, six wavelengths away, are the test bodies. Speeds are measured against how fast a singer can re-time itself.

With one fixed timing, heat weakens the pull. With revision 19’s rule, a quarter beat ahead, the ball at rest sings 4.4 times louder than independent singers and pulls twice as hard. Moving freely at random breaks the chorus and halves the pull (0.36–0.49 of rest); collisions keep it (0.87–0.96), exactly as Dicke narrowing says. The reason is exact: a body is pulled by the recoil of what it feeds the passing wave (pull = power fed ÷ wave speed), and bodies in step all feed or all absorb together. So a pull needs a loud chorus, which motion can only break. We checked 16 timings: no exception. The opposite timing hushes the ball, to a fifth of independent singers’ volume, and motion releases the hush up to four times, which is the heat term’s pattern, but its test bodies are pushed.

Another solution: feed quiet waves, absorb loud ones. One rule for every singer: a quarter beat ahead of a quiet wave and a quarter beat behind a loud one, the way an amplifier with a small built-in loss boosts a faint signal but soaks up a loud one. Inside a dense ball the waves are loud, so it hushes itself; far away they are quiet, so the test bodies feed and are pulled.

Revision 23: the timing rule from a power balance

Round 13’s rule worked but was put in by hand. Give each piece of matter a fixed supply of power, our law’s 5.3 × 10−6 watts per kilogram, and a loss that grows with the loudness of the wave around it. Whatever it doesn’t lose, it must feed into the wave, and that fixes its timing, the way the power balance decides whether a machine on the electrical grid runs as a generator or a motor: ahead of a quiet wave (it feeds the wave and is pulled), behind a loud one (it draws from the wave). That is the rule, from two physical numbers.

  • The heat pattern comes back, with no memory: free random motion strengthens the pull (×1.30–1.81 at slow speeds), frequent collisions hold the gain back (×0.98–1.08), rotation gives none; stopped, every ball returns to its cold, dark state.
  • A singer that simply absorbs as well also goes dark and still pulls, but motion can’t free what an absorber holds (×1.04–1.07). The hush has to be arranged by the singers’ own timing.
  • The release factor may be the same loss. Held back in loud waves, the singers give a shape the 149 galaxies accept as well as our exponential: a typical miss of 15.86 and 16.08 km/s against 15.87.
  • Darkness sets where heat takes over, at about four times the cold darkness in units of the re-timing speed, and test bodies keep step up to about the re-timing speed. So the darker cold matter is, the wider the range covered; real matter can be far darker than our 100 singers.
  • Still to solve: the steepness. After a steep start the release grows about 1.6 times per doubling of speed; our law, and the lensing data, need about 4 times.
  • A gentler heat term, σ1.75 instead of σ², on the full test suite: 60 pass, 10 close, 7 fail against 59, 11, 7. It closes galaxy lensing’s common 16% excess but makes the lensing of massive ellipticals worse, so σ² stays. Two analyses of the same KiDS survey pull in opposite directions here.

The inverted ringers run 10–20% above the square root (×2.41, 4.38, 8.24 where it is 2.00, 3.61, 7.00). It is one cloud seen from outside, not yet the full law.
  • Which bodies are pulled: a self-sustained body that holds energy ready to give, like the atoms of a laser, falls into step a quarter beat ahead of a passing wave by itself and is pulled by the wave’s height. That is our round-10 rule, now from a body’s own equations; an independent audit reproduced it. It is the firmest result of the round. An ordinary one, the calculation’s included, falls a quarter beat behind and is pushed. Matter holds an enormous store, its rest energy, so the first kind is the natural one.
  • Collisions hold it back twice: at the source the extra glow falls as 1/(1 + collision rate), to within 1%; and bodies too slow to follow the flickering waves lose step. Stopped, the extra pull vanishes; only the spent fuel is gone.
  • The wave alone gives half of it: the symmetries and the collision rule, but not the σ²: the cloud we tested has no clean edge between quiet and loud. A quiet store inside each piece of matter is a concrete candidate that would have one. The same missing edge is a likely reason round 14’s version fell short.
  • The energy didn’t balance yet (found by the audit): the pieces’ waves interfere, so together they carried 0.6 to 1.4 times the energy the pieces lost. Revision 25 fixes this (below).
  • The energy bill (corrected in revision 25): being pulled costs power, set by how fast the wave’s crests move. The galaxies allow crests up to half the companion’s travel speed, so the bill is at most what matter gives off when cold. Light-speed crests would cost 3,500 times more, and the planets’ orbits already rule that out.
  • Next:
    • make every piece of matter both a source and a receiver, with the shared wave’s energy balanced from the start (moved up after the audit);
    • find the physical quiet store (a pair of internal vibrations that motion pulls apart, as an electric field mixes a quiet atomic state into a bright one) and derive the companion’s speed from it;
    • see whether a receiver drained in strong waves switches its pull off, which would be our release factor.

Step 2: what opens the quiet store? The companion flowing past. The independent calculation says motion opens each piece’s quiet store, but not what the motion is measured against. We tried the simplest answer: against the companion itself, which streams out of all matter at 169 km/s. A piece sitting still then still feels the companion rushing past, so it leaks a little even at rest: that is the cold glow. Random motion adds exactly our law’s heat weight, factor 3 and all, with the same 169 km/s (figure, left). Collisions hold it back as the law needs (5.90, 2.96, 1.07, 0.38 against 6, 3, 1.09, 0.39).

  • Why it matters: our law uses one speed for two jobs, how fast the companion travels and how much random motion counts as heat. Here they are the same speed automatically.
  • Consequences: a spinning disk counts as cold only if the companion moves along with its matter, the memory the Bullet Cluster already needs; and matter falling inward, against the outflow, should glow more.
  • The crossing heat: galaxies ploughing through another cluster’s companion are heated by the relative motion. In the Bullet Cluster, the smaller half’s lensing mass rises from 1.39 to 1.56–1.64 × 1014 suns (measured 2.47–2.85), and the main half moves into its measured range (3.07–3.16; measured 3.09–3.46). A real help, not the whole answer.
  • Status: conditional on the quiet-store idea itself.

Step 3a: the shared wave with its energy balanced. We rebuilt the wave’s coupling between pieces completely. It has two halves: one carries energy away, and the near field shuffles it between close neighbours. The power through a distant sphere now equals what the pieces lose, to 1 part in 100,000, and two pieces a tenth of a wavelength apart radiate 1.9355 and 0.0645 times their own share in and out of step, the audit’s numbers.

  • With only the energy-carrying half (the audit’s version) a crowded cloud chokes itself: each piece’s radiators re-radiate against their neighbours’ quiet glow and cut it to under 5%, and the doubling rule fails (figure, right: the line that drops).
  • With both halves, as the wave really has them, the choking mostly goes: 82–100% of the cold glow remains, and doubling the speed spread multiplies the extra glow by 3.3–3.9 in packed clouds and 4.0 in spread-out ones, until the fastest stores run low. Collisions leave 52% and 9% of the extra where the simple formula says 50% and 9.1%.
  • Packed clouds glow less for the same motion (4.3 times the cold glow instead of 12, for example): in our law, a slightly larger u for tightly packed matter.

Step 3b: one kind of matter, every piece both sender and receiver. The audit’s main recommendation, and the real test. Until now our sources and our test bodies were different kinds of matter. Now every piece is the same: a quiet oscillation that holds energy ready to give (the kind that falls into step ahead of a passing wave by itself), a finite store that keeps it going, and three radiators that motion stirs. A ball of 48 pieces is the source; 8 more of exactly the same matter sit farther out as receivers; all talk through the complete wave. Nothing sets anyone’s timing, nothing turns power into force, and there is no switch for strong fields.

  • What works: the books balance (energy to about 1 part in a trillion); a cold source pulls distant pieces of the same matter, which fall into step ahead of its wave by themselves; and a warming source glows as the heat rule says, while the wave reaching the receivers grows as the square root of that glow (×1.01, 1.31, 2.24, 2.81 for glow ×1.2, 2.1, 5.0, 8.1). The square root our law needs is there in the wave.
  • A new exact rule: a piece pulled by feeding a wave loses part of the pull to its own radiators, which the same wave stirs. The net is (1 − 2f) of the full pull, with f the share of the radiators’ energy that goes into the companion. If they sent it all into the companion, a feeding piece would be pushed. So matter’s loud vibrations must mostly ring inside and only whisper into the companion: a requirement that came out of the equations.
  • Not yet: keeping step with warm matter. As the source warms, the receivers fall out of step (0.55 → 0.16–0.27), so their pull stays flat instead of growing (figure), and their radiators are pushed harder. The audit’s decisive test is not passed yet.
  • Why, measured: warming puts the source’s own pieces out of tune with each other (the spread of their rhythms grows eightfold). A smeared beat can’t be followed from a distance. Frequent collisions keep the beat, and then the receivers keep step and are pulled as by a cold source, matching our law’s rule that colliding gas behaves as cold.
  • Two follow-ups. Radiators that whisper, a twentieth of their energy into the companion, shrink the push four to sevenfold, so the receivers are pulled at every heat; but the pull still doesn’t grow. And a cold source with no heat at all, put out of tune by hand by the same amounts, loses the receivers in just the same way. The cause is confirmed.
  • Given time, a cold source’s pull becomes nearly perfect: run three times longer, its pieces settle into one beat, and the distant pieces lock onto it a quarter beat ahead, by themselves, at 0.91–0.92 on a scale where 1 is perfect. A warm source never settles.
  • Where this leaves us: moving matter glows more as σ²; the glow travels as waves and reaches distant matter as its square root; matter that holds energy ready to give falls into step ahead of the wave by itself and is pulled; and the pull pays its own bill. One link is missing, and it is now a single, measurable question: how does warm matter keep its pieces in tune closely enough for distant matter to follow its wave? Cold matter does, and colliding matter does. That is what we work on next.

Revision 26: keeping warm matter in tune

Revision 25 ended with one missing link: a warm source’s pieces fall out of tune, so distant matter can’t follow its wave and the pull doesn’t grow with the heat. An independent review set this round’s goal: find a reason, inside the physics, why making more companion doesn’t wreck the beat the pull depends on. It proposed a candidate: give each piece a matched pair of inner responses whose pulls on its beat cancel.

What knocks a warm piece out of tune. A piece’s loud inner vibrations react a thousand times faster than its beat drifts, so they can be solved for exactly and taken out, leaving one equation for every piece’s beat. We checked it two ways: in the full simulation each beat is exactly the sum of three pushes, and the short equation gives the same results as the full simulation. The pieces push on each other’s beats in two ways. One is like the pull of a common rhythm, and on its own it always lets a group settle. The other is a sideways push that depends on where the neighbours are in their own beats, and on its own it can keep a group churning forever. Switching parts off by hand (figure, left) shows that the sideways half of the pushes between warm pieces does all the damage. With only the other half, distant matter keeps step at 0.88, better than with a cold source (0.63), because the warm source’s wave is stronger.

Can a piece protect itself? We reproduced the review’s pair exactly: no shift of the beat at all, a 2% shift for a 1% mismatch, 80% of the glow kept under collisions. But in one kind of matter both halves of the pair shine into the same shared wave, and then the cancellation doesn’t happen. We tried twelve inner structures, all balancing their energy, at equal glow, and found a limit: the pushes come out the same size in every one, because whatever lets a piece send its glow into the wave lets it receive its neighbours’ glow just as strongly. Two things still help:

  • Motion has to enter the way a velocity does. A velocity flips sign when time runs backwards, and a push proportional to it must then take a particular form, the form of the Coriolis force. Round 16 used the other form. With the right one a whole class of offsets vanishes exactly, distant matter keeps step better (0.18 → 0.33 at a heat weight of 8), and in the full simulation the pull of a warm source’s wave on distant matter now grows with the heat (×1.22 of the cold value at a heat weight of 8, where round 16’s form gave ×0.85).
  • The review’s pair, spread far apart in pitch, makes each piece a poor echo of its neighbours while it still shines when it moves: 0.41 together with the velocity’s form, and 0.83 for a cold source.
  • A second requirement: a source’s whole beat must stay exactly where cold matter’s is. One structure kept a warm source perfectly together but on a slightly shifted beat, and distant matter, whose hold weakens with distance, couldn’t follow.

A wave that only travels outward. Everything points at the two-way traffic between warm pieces: each one’s wave coming back to it off its neighbours, every pair pushing each other back and forth. What would stop it is a wave that can’t come back. Our companion streams outward from the matter that makes it. If its crests are carried outward by that stream faster than they can move against it, a piece hears only matter nearer the centre, and never its own echo. We tested exactly that (figure, right):

  • Warmth no longer spoils the beat. A warm source’s pieces share one beat (70 times tighter than with the two-way wave), and distant matter keeps step at 0.98, 0.96, 0.85 and 0.81 at four distances, better the warmer the source. Measured against what our law needs, the pull’s growth with heat comes out 1.1 to 1.5 at every distance, where the two-way wave gives 0.2 to 0.8.
  • What matters is that the wave has no way back, not its direction: with the pieces put in a random one-way order, each hearing only the ones before it, it works just as well (1.00, 0.99, 0.81, 0.84). The outflow supplies the natural order, from the centre outward.
  • It has to be nearly one-way: a 10% leak back inward keeps most of it, 30% loses it.
  • The glow stays in proportion to mass (the same per piece for sources of 24, 48 and 96 pieces), as our law needs.
  • A bonus: with a wave that only travels outward, a piece feels only the matter nearer the centre than itself. For a round source that is Newton’s rule that only the mass inside a radius pulls there, the form our law already takes.
  • What it is: a test of an idea, made by hand in the short equations. Making it real means working out the companion as a flowing medium: how fast its crests move against its own outflow.

The dwarf galaxies

What the Cassini fix opens

Six of the ten dwarf galaxies around the Milky Way come out too slow, five of them by a factor of 2.7–5. Why: inside a dwarf, our law’s extra pull depends on the square root of the total pull there, and that includes the Milky Way’s. For the faintest dwarfs the Milky Way’s pull is 2 to 80 times their own. Under a square root a big steady pull swamps a small one, like a whisper added to a shout, so the dwarf’s own companion counts for little. Taking the Milky Way’s pull out of each dwarf’s formula takes the combined miss (χ²) from 135 to 80; taking its heat out as well brings it to 60. MOND with the same stars scores 119.

What stood in the way: dropping the hold on dwarfs also drops it on pairs of stars, which would then pull three times harder than Newton’s law. What changed: the companion now builds up over a short distance around each star. If that distance is half a parsec or more, still far too small to touch any galaxy, pairs of stars 20,000 AU apart stay at 1.02–1.36 times Newton’s pull even with no hold. The Milky Way

Our own galaxy, star by star

The Milky Way is measured in more detail than any other galaxy, so it is the hardest test. We built its visible matter from published maps (McMillan 2017: bulge, two stellar disks, two gas disks and the thin halo of old stars) and ran the law with nothing adjusted.

How the law sees it. Inside about 3 kpc the pull is stronger than g_d, the companion is held back and gravity is nearly Newton’s. At the Sun the ordinary pull is 1.3 × 10−10 m/s², just under g_d, so about half the companion is released: the Sun sits right in the switch-over. Beyond about 15 kpc it is fully released, and the extra pull fades slowly.

  • Far out, our law follows the Gaia measurements. The standard dark-matter model of the Galaxy stays near 220 km/s there, 6–11% too fast.
  • Near the Sun it is 8% slow — and that is not special to the Milky Way. At the same strength of pull the typical SPARC galaxy also sits 7% above our law.   • The pull above the disk is right with the stars and gas actually counted near the Sun (McKee et al. 2015): 73 against 68–74. MOND gives 84. Together with the rotation, this says the extra pull near the Sun should lean a little more towards the Galaxy’s centre — the “direction-dependent gravity” idea, now with a number attached.
  • The Galaxy’s mass out to 100 and 200 kpc agrees with our law; MOND’s is 10–40% high there.

Why the six faint dwarfs are slow, and what would change it. In our law, as in MOND, a dwarf sitting in the Galaxy’s field gets much less extra pull. Without that hold, Crater II comes out at 3.4 km/s (measured 2.7) and Antlia 2 at 4.2 (measured 5.7). Revision 18 found that the Cassini fix makes dropping that hold possible (see “What the Cassini fix opens”); why the hold would be weaker is still to be found. Draco and Ursa Minor would still need about twice their stars; they are hard cases for MOND too, and are thought to be disturbed by the Galaxy’s tides. Published MOND numbers for these dwarfs used a rougher formula that gives about 1.7 times higher speeds.

Lensing by galaxies

Ellipticals bend light harder, and the lenses agree

Ellipticals versus spirals. The KiDS survey (Brouwer et al. 2021) found elliptical galaxies bend light more than spirals with the same stars, by 0.17 dex splitting by shape and 0.27 dex splitting by colour, at more than 5.7σ. A law that treats all matter alike cannot do that. In ours, an elliptical’s stars move randomly at 150–200 km/s and never collide, so they are hot; a spiral’s stars circle in step. Our prediction: 0.17 to 0.27 dex, roughly flat beyond 100 kpc, with no hidden gas needed. Script kids_v3.py.

Six strong lenses (SLACS). With standard distances and published star masses, the stars need to be 1.05–1.35 times heavier than the “Salpeter” standard — the heavy populations that spectra of giant ellipticals already suggest (1.32–1.78 times in our own distances, revision 21). The mass needed to bend the light and the mass needed to move the stars agree to −0.017 ± 0.024 dex (−0.028 ± 0.023 in ours, revision 21): light and matter feel the same pull.

  • Microlensing towards the bulge is standard: at a lensing star’s ring, about 3 AU across, the pull is a million times g_d. The event rate counts stars, as observed.
  • Colliding clusters beyond the Bullet — Abell 520 with its disputed “dark core”, MACS J0025, El Gordo — and the dense cluster Abell 1689: the published measurements are collected, ready to model next.

The Solar System. The release factor switches the extra pull off at every planet. The Sun’s interior is colliding plasma, so it has no heat term; it feeds the companion 2.8% of its light, an extra mass loss of 1.9 × 10−15 per year, below what planetary tracking can see. The star S2 around the Galaxy’s central black hole shows exactly Einstein’s precession, as measured (1.10 ± 0.19); binary pulsars decay exactly as Einstein says, and our tiny mass loss is 0.3–1.3% of their measurement error.

Cassini: failed by the law as it stood, passed since revision 18. In laws like ours and MOND, the Galaxy’s pull slightly reshapes the Sun’s own extra pull thousands of AU out, and that reshaping reaches the planets as a tiny stretching force, Q2. Nine years of radio tracking of Cassini at Saturn measured Q2 = (3 ± 3) × 10−27 s−2 (Hees et al. 2014). The law as it stood gave 2.4–3.1 × 10−26, about ten times too big, the same as MOND. The fix: breaking free takes time. The companion leaving the Sun is released gradually over 0.15 parsec of travel, about 840 years at 169 km/s. Q2 drops to 4.4 × 10−27, inside the measurement. Every galaxy, dwarf and cluster is thousands of times larger, so none of them changes. The full regression suite confirms it: Cassini moves from fail to pass, and no other graded check changes its grade. Revision 18 adopts it. It is “gravity that builds up over time”, now with a measured length. Scripts strong_field_v7.py, release_hold_v9.py.

Wide binary stars: a prediction. Pairs of stars thousands of AU apart pull on each other weakly, below the level where galaxies stop following Newton. Gaia analyses disagree: Chae reports about 40% extra pull; Banik and colleagues report none. A pair near the Sun sits in the Galaxy’s own pull, which holds back half of the companion. Solving our field equation exactly for a pair in that pull gives 19% extra pull beyond about 7,000 AU (orbits about 9% faster), against MOND’s 43%. It switches on later than MOND, because the release factor keeps close pairs Newtonian. Our law sits between the two published answers and agrees with neither, which makes this a clean test. Revision 18: with the Cassini fix adopted, the prediction is 4% at 7,000 AU and 9% at 20,000 AU, locked in revision 19. Predictions anyone can test

  1. Heavy stars in the six SLACS lenses: 1.37–1.85 times Salpeter with the project’s own distances (1.05–1.35 with the standard ones), measurable from their spectra; dark-matter models expect about 1.0.
  2. The elliptical/spiral lensing gap is flat beyond 100 kpc; a hot-gas-halo explanation makes it grow.
  3. In relaxed clusters, galaxy orbits and the gas agree: both feel the same pull.
  4. In every collision, lensing stays with the galaxies at every stage.
  5. The Bullet Cluster’s smaller half carries a companion heated during its crossing, so its lensing is strong although few galaxies travel with it.
  6. The Bullet Cluster’s outskirts hold stars at about 5% of the gas mass at the cluster’s edge (confirmed by the star count).
  7. Wide binary stars: 4% more pull than Newton at 7,000 AU and 9% at 20,000 AU, with the release length adopted in revision 18: a fifth of MOND’s 43%. Locked in the repository before Gaia’s next data release; 3.5% and 8% with revision 20’s constants, 3.2% and 7.6% with revision 21’s, within the locked window’s errors.
  8. Lensing in a collision shows the clusters as they were before it. Around stopped gas, lensing comes back only inside a sphere growing at 169 km/s, about 170 kpc per billion years.
  9. Older collisions show extra lensing around the smaller clump, as stream heat and tidal shaking build up.
  10. At equal visible mass, systems whose stars move randomly and freely pull harder than those whose stars circle in step.
  11. The Sun’s extra mass loss: 1.9 × 10−15 per year.
  12. The Milky Way keeps slowing beyond 25 kpc: about 189 km/s at 30 kpc and 179 at 50 kpc.
  13. Lensing speeds around isolated galaxies stay flat to about 1.7–2.2 Mpc, then fall, where the companion has not yet reached.
  14. Compact lenses like the Einstein Cross need only their stars inside the ring.
  15. Ellipticals and spirals follow two different lensing curves, set by how randomly their stars move (KiDS agrees).
  16. The far clusters’ published star masses are about right (revision 20; revision 19’s “1.4–2 times heavier, older stars” is withdrawn): with the companion’s speed measured in our own distances, El Gordo’s lensing agrees with its published stars.
  17. Abell 520’s galaxy-poor clump carries about 3 × 1013 suns inside 150 kpc, a quarter of it visible matter, mostly gas, with a lensing peak on the gas and almost no galaxies.
  18. MACS J0025’s clusters crossed less than about 400 million years ago; otherwise, in our law, its north-western lensing peak would sit on the gas, where none is seen. Its radio relics and its separation date the crossing at 0.1–0.36 billion years (revision 21).
  19. Isolated galaxies are wrapped in gas weighing about as much as their stars within 100 kpc, more around ellipticals than around spirals. With that gas our law matches KiDS’s lensing in our own distances. (Revision 20’s version, that galaxy lensing measures the distance law’s rate, is withdrawn.)

How we got here

Seventeen rounds, with the corrections kept

  • Revisions 1–9 used a streaming-gravity law that turned out to be MOND’s, plus a light-versus-matter “slip”; the law was retired under the new rules, and the slip proved an artefact of measuring against MOND.
  • Revision 10 (round 1): hot-companion gravity with the gas as the hot part. It fit galaxies and clusters but put the Bullet’s lensing on the gas.
  • Revision 11 (round 2): MOND derived as the cold limit; a field equation; the Bullet only partly solved.
  • Revision 12 (round 3): the collision rule and the flow direction. Stars carry the heat; the Bullet’s pattern is reproduced.
  • Revision 13 (round 4): the companion’s memory, safe now that the companion is slow; the 72-collision stack still holds.
  • Revision 14 (round 5): the law piece by piece; the main cluster’s galaxy speed and lensing mass matched.
  • Revision 15 (round 6): a star count confirms the main cluster’s outskirts; the smaller half’s missing stars are not found, so its lensing strength is open again; the wide-binary prediction.
  • Revision 16 (round 7): the full check against the Milky Way and the standard stars and lenses. Most pass, including galaxy lensing of both kinds (better than MOND); three shortfalls, each with a testable fix.
  • Revision 17 (round 8): a regression suite that re-runs every test in one command; three more collisions (MACS J0025 passes; Abell 520’s dark core from gas and heat; El Gordo’s stars to measure). Correction: with the Cassini fix, wide binaries get 9% extra pull at 20,000 AU, not 1–5%.
  • Revision 18 (round 9): the Cassini fix adopted after the full suite showed it costs nothing (62 pass, 8 close, 6 fail). The same change opens a way for the dwarfs: 6 of 10 agree without the Milky Way’s hold, the reason still to be found. An audit for borrowed assumptions, and ten proposals for finishing the theory.
  • Revision 19 (round 10): the pull derived from a local interaction and checked by a 3D simulation; the way the companion adds up tested against two alternatives, and only the law’s own fits both galaxies and clusters. The far clusters and strong lenses in the project’s own distances, now graded that way (58 pass, 11 close, 7 fail); the grades that slipped all trace to lighter stars. A locked wide-binary forecast.
  • Revision 20 (round 11): how orderly matter adds up, derived from energy conservation. The calibrating clusters’ stars corrected (counted in circles on the sky since round 1) and every test in the project’s own distances; the companion’s speed measured again at 163 km/s, and the far clusters recover without heavier stars. Galaxy lensing becomes a test of our distance law’s scale and of the companion’s speed (57 pass, 9 close, 10 fail).
  • Revision 21 (round 12): revision 20’s next steps, one at a time. MACS J0025’s age read from its shock fronts: young, and both lensing peaks on their galaxies. The lensing galaxies’ star speeds measured: the ellipticals’ extra lensing matches. The distance law’s rate fitted jointly to supernovae and the galaxies, 5% lower, with every data set in our own distances; galaxy lensing’s remaining level points to the gas around the lenses. A working model of the companion: only a companion guided along gravity’s field lines keeps every watt, travels as one stream and makes no whirlpools (59 pass, 11 close, 7 fail).
  • Revision 22 (round 13): the pull and the scrambling in one experiment. With one fixed timing, free random motion halves the pull, for an exact reason. Emitters that feed quiet waves but absorb loud ones give the heat term’s pattern at slow speeds: up to twice the pull moving freely, held back by collisions, none from rotation, with our law’s square root emerging by itself. Galaxy lensing pins the heat term’s steepness: doubling the speed spread gives 1.8–2.0 times the extra pull.
  • Revision 23 (round 14): the next steps, one at a time. A gentler heat term on the full suite trades one tension for another, so σ² stays (and a slip in the suite’s refit was fixed). “Feed quiet waves, absorb loud ones” derived from a power balance, with no memory; its hold in loud waves fits the galaxies as well as our release factor.
