Hot-companion gravity · a visual guide · 26 September 2026

Gravity's currents

In the hot-companion law, every piece of ordinary matter sends out a faint companion to its gravity. The companion drifts outward at 169 km/s and keeps the motion of the matter that made it. Where more of it gathers, gravity pulls harder. Drawn like wind on a weather map, it shows why the law works for the Solar System, galaxies, clusters and colliding clusters, and what gravity would still have to do where the data disagree.

The Bullet Cluster: two galaxy clusters that passed through each other about 200 million years ago at 3,000–3,900 km/s, seen in the frame where their combined centre stands still (one second of animation is 16 million years). Each streak is a piece of the companion; the density of streaks shows how strongly it pulls. The motions come from a simple straight-line model. The blue contours are the law's real lensing map of today, from the same 3D calculation the test suite grades.

How to read the maps

Four rules, four colours

Orderly matter feeds the companion in stepPale streaks. Stars on steady orbits and gas send out companion that adds up the way Newton's pulls do, like arrows. Its pull falls off as 1/distance instead of 1/distance², so it takes over far from the centre.

Randomly moving matter glows much brighterAmber streaks. Galaxies in a cluster move at about 1,000 km/s, six times faster than the companion, and make about 100 times more of it. That glow adds up like brightness from every direction.

Gas makes no extra glowPink haze. Hot gas is fast too, but its particles collide billions of times, so they cannot keep a random motion going long enough to glow. In a collision this is what separates the pull from the gas.

Strong gravity holds it back; it keeps its source's motionNear a star the companion's pull is switched off, so the Solar System feels nothing extra. And after a collision the companion keeps going with the galaxies, even where the gas has stopped.

Part 1

Where the currents match the measurements

The Solar System: a calm eye

What gravity doesStrong gravity holds the companion back. Where Newton's pull is stronger than 2 × 10−10 m/s², the companion's pull is switched off by a factor e−pull ÷ (2×10−10). The Sun's pull falls to that value about 5,400 times the Earth–Sun distance out (5,400 AU). At the Earth the factor is e−29,000,000: zero, to any precision anyone can measure.

What the data sayThe planets, spacecraft and the bending of starlight follow Newton and Einstein exactly: the extra pull on the planets is below 10−12 of Newton's (it is zero), and the 2026 Cassini measurement of the shape of the Sun's field passes (4.4 against 1.6 ± 1.8 × 10−27 s−2). Far out, the Galaxy's own pull keeps part of the hold on, so pairs of stars 20,000 AU apart get 7.6% more pull, between the two published analyses (none, and about 1.5 times).

How much of the companion's pull gets through

A spinning galaxy: the sprinkler current

What gravity doesStars and gas in a disk galaxy move in orderly circles, so their companions add up the way Newton's pulls do. The law's extra pull is √(a × Newton's pull), with a = 6.3 × 10−11 m/s². It falls off as 1/distance instead of 1/distance², so far from the centre it takes over: in NGC 3198 it supplies 77% of the pull at 30 kpc. Each streak also keeps the spin of the star that made it, so the current leaves the disk in a spiral, like water from a lawn sprinkler.

What the data sayNGC 3198 spins at 146–150 km/s from 30 to 44 kpc; the law gives 147–150, and the visible matter alone (Newton) only 66–70. The faint, gas-rich galaxy DDO 154: measured 46 km/s, law 44–45, Newton 16–19. Across all 149 galaxies the typical miss is 15.9 km/s, slightly better than MOND's 16.1 with the same data. Switch the stars to Newton's speeds to see how far the outer disk would fall behind.

A galaxy cluster: the hot glow adds up like brightness

What gravity doesGalaxies in a cluster move at about 1,000 km/s in random directions. Matter moving that way makes far more companion: the heat weight k = 3σ²/u² is about 100 for cluster galaxies. Their glow reaches every point from all directions and adds up like light from many lamps, whichever way it comes from. The gas makes none. The probe compares that brightness with the glow's net flow, where arrows from opposite sides cancel: near the centre the brightness is highest while the arrows almost vanish.

What the data sayIn Abell 1795 the visible matter gives only 9–15% of the mass the X-rays measure. The law, with the glow as brightness, stays within about 30% at every radius; across the 12 X-COP clusters the typical miss is about 25%, against about 190% for MOND. Adding the glow as arrows, as the round-24 field equation first did, leaves the inner cluster 40–53% short and more than doubles the typical miss. One added term, the glow's non-flowing brightness, repaired that equation.

Abell 1795: mass inside each radius

Colliding clusters: the current keeps going with the galaxies

What gravity doesThree things put the pull on the galaxies in the map at the top. The gas makes no extra glow. The galaxies' own hot glow is by far the brightest part of the companion. And the companion keeps the motion of the matter that made it, so what the gas made before the crossing still travels with the galaxies (the memory). The first two do most of the work; the memory adds 5–10% on the galaxies and takes a little off the gas.

What the data sayIn the Bullet Cluster the law puts the lensing peaks 16 and 23 kpc from the galaxies, while the gas lies 230–250 kpc behind; the leftover lensing on the gas is 0.05, as measured (0.05 ± 0.06). In 72 other collisions (Harvey et al. 2015) the lensing follows the gas by 0.02 of its lag, against −0.04 ± 0.07 measured. Abell 520's galaxy-poor “dark core” comes from its dense gas plus the surrounding glow, El Gordo's lensing masses pass, and MACS J0025's lensing peaks sit on its galaxies.

Part 2

Where gravity has to do more

Seven checks fail and eleven are close. They fall into five problems. For each: what gravity does in the law now, and what it would have to do instead.

1 · The Bullet's smaller half needs 1.8 times more pull

What the data sayLensing measures 2.47–2.85 × 1014 suns within 300 kpc of the smaller cluster's galaxies (in the project's distances). The law gives 1.38, about 55%. The main cluster is fine (2.97, within errors of 3.09–3.46), and so is where the lensing sits.

What gravity needs to doPull about 1.8 times harder within 300 kpc of the smaller cluster's galaxies, but not on its gas, less than 250 million years after the crossing.

The leadThe smaller cluster's galaxies ploughed through the big cluster's companion at 3,000–3,900 km/s. That shakes them and makes extra glow (1.38 → 1.59). Glow spreading at 169 km/s covers only about 37 kpc in 220 million years, though, while lensing counts everything out to 300 kpc. If it spreads at about 600 km/s and the collision puts about 2.2 times more power into it (driven by the flicker where the two clusters' companions interfere), the smaller half reaches 2.4–2.5 and the main cluster stays in range. Tick “Add the proposed collision glow” in the map at the top. The speed and the power still have to be derived from how matter stores and releases energy.

2 · Faint dwarf galaxies near the Milky Way: a current swamped by a river

What the data sayFive of the ten dwarf galaxies nearest us have stars moving 2–5 times faster than the law predicts: Draco 9.1 km/s measured against 2.8.

What gravity does in the lawA dwarf sits in the Milky Way's current, which near Draco is about 15 times stronger than Draco's own at its strongest. The law adds the two before deciding how strong the companion's pull is, so the Galaxy decides and the dwarf's own extra pull is diluted. The streaks show that net flow: it runs straight past the dwarf.

What gravity needs to doLet a dwarf keep more of its own current. Leaving the Galaxy out of a dwarf's own rule (tested in round 20) brings Carina and Antlia 2 into range and Sextans and Crater II close, while the Solar System and wide binaries still pass. Draco and Ursa Minor still need about twice the speed; they are the closest of the ten (76–78 kpc), where the Galaxy's pull and tides are strongest. Next step: derive how a small system's companion responds to an outside current, from the same physics that sets the hold.

3 · Galaxy lensing: about 16% more pull than predicted

What the data sayWeak lensing around 259,000 isolated galaxies (KiDS-1000) shows about 16% more pull 100 kpc to 1 Mpc out than the law gives; 20% for red galaxies, 2–3% for blue galaxies and the spectroscopic sample. The law does get the difference between red and blue galaxies right (0.13 against 0.15 ± 0.04 on the log scale), so the heat term works here; the overall level is off.

What gravity needs to doPull about 16% harder around isolated galaxies far out, most of all around red elliptical galaxies.

The leadsThe distance law: a slowly growing path factor fitted to supernovae removes most of the gap and lifts the suite to 62 passes (round 21, registered). And the hot gas around elliptical galaxies, which is not counted yet.

Measured lensing above the law's prediction

4 · The Milky Way at the Sun: 9% too slow

What the data sayThe law gives 209 km/s at the Sun's orbit; four recent Gaia analyses give 229–234.

What gravity does in the lawThe Galaxy's own pull at the Sun's orbit is close to the hold scale, so the hold switches off about half of the companion's pull there. Without the hold the speed would be 234 km/s, in range, but the Solar System needs the hold.

What gravity needs to doHold the companion completely near stars but a little more gently at the scale of a galaxy's disk. The hold's exact shape is not derived yet; round 9 fixed only where it ends (0.15 pc from each star), and the adopted shape is the simplest one that fits the galaxies.

5 · Galaxies in merging clusters: 15–20% faster than predicted

What the data sayMACS J0025: 669 km/s against 835 ± 59; El Gordo's north-west half: 1,014 against 1,290 ± 134; Abell 520's clumps are similar. All are close, none fails.

What gravity needs to doProbably nothing new. The calculation treats each cluster as settled, while a merger speeds its galaxies up for a while. The mergers' own motion has to be modelled before this counts for or against the law.

Summary

What gravity is doing, and what it still has to do

Every success comes from the same four rules. Every problem asks for one of two things: a stronger or faster glow where matter has just been shaken, or a gentler hold in weak outside fields. Each is a concrete calculation.
